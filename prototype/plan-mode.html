Step 3 — The lesson, step by step

Not an outline of what a lesson should contain — what actually happens, in order, with the words to say, the questions to ask, and what to watch for. Deep enough to build slides from.

Lesson 3 of 6 · Y3 Science · Rocks · 60 min
AI-drafted — check before teaching
Learning objective
Compare two rocks and explain which is better suited to a job.
Success criteria
  • I can describe two rocks using property words. Evidence: completed comparison table
  • I can test which rock is harder and which lets water through. Evidence: two ticked columns with a reason
  • I can choose a rock for a job and give a property as my reason. Evidence: written sentence using “because”

Vocabulary — three bands, not one list

You said “hard, soft, rough” is too easy for some and too hard for others. So every lesson names all three bands, and the plan says who each is for and when to introduce it.

Everyone leaves with
Including new-to-English arrivals
hard · soft · rough · smooth · shiny · dull
Expected
Most of the class, taught explicitly today
grainy · layered · absorbent · waterproof · scratch
Stretch
Offered to all, expected of some
permeable · impermeable · porous · durable
How the bands are used: teach the everyday word first, then name the technical one beside it — “lets water through — scientists call that permeable.” A child using “lets water through” has met the criterion. Using “permeable” exceeds it.

The lesson, in order

1 · Hook3 min
On the board Photograph of a waterlogged pitch, standing water on the surface, match abandoned.
“This game was called off. Not because of the players — because of what's underneath the grass.”
Ask: “What do you think is under a football pitch?” Take three answers. Don't correct any of them yet.
Reveal: the layers — grass, sand, gravel, stone.
Then: “By the end of today, you'll choose the right rock for a job like this one.”
2 · Retrieval — and the re-teach7 min
Four rocks from Lesson 2 on each table. Ask: “Name one. Tell your partner one property.” Cold-call three.
Expect: granite — hard · chalk — soft · sandstone — grainy.
Re-teach permeability — this is why the lesson opens here. A pipette of water on granite, a pipette on sandstone. Everyone watches for twenty seconds. Ask: “Did the water sit on top, or go in?”
Watch for: children saying a rock “soaked it up” because the surface looks wet. Lift it and show the underside — wet surface isn't the same as water going through.
3 · I do — modelling10 min
Compare granite and chalk aloud, filling row 1 of the table on the board while they watch.
“Granite is hard — I can't scratch it with my nail. Chalk is soft — look, it marks the paper straight away. So if something is going to be walked on all day, I want the hard one.”
Model the sentence: “I would choose granite because it is hard.”
Model a wrong answer on purpose, then fix it. “I could choose chalk because it's pretty.” — then: “Pretty isn't a property that helps here. My reason has to be something I tested.” This is the error most of them will make.
4 · We do — together5 min
Row 2 together, sandstone and slate. Children write in their own tables as you fill the board.
Ask: “Which one would you not want on a roof? Say why using a property word.”
5 · You do — paired testing25 min
Pairs, four rocks, comparison table. Scratch test first, then the water test. Then the “because” sentence.
Adults: TA at the water station — it's the messy one and the one they rush. You target the pairs who found permeability hard last lesson.
Watch for: pairs testing both rocks with different amounts of water. Stop and ask: “Is that a fair test?”
6 · Plenary10 min
Pick two children who chose different rocks. Both justify.
“They chose different rocks and they're both right — because they were thinking about different jobs.”
Exit question: “Would you use chalk for a playground? Why not?”
Timings: 3 + 7 + 10 + 5 + 25 + 10 = 60 minutes. Checked automatically — a plan whose parts don't add up to the lesson is rejected.

Same objective for everyone — what changes is the route

Nobody gets a smaller objective. The adaptations change how a child reaches it and how they show it.

EAL — access

  • Pre-teach band 1 words with the actual rocks in hand, before the lesson if possible
  • Stem on the table: “The ___ is harder than the ___ because ___.”
  • Word mat with a photograph beside every property word
  • Talk to a partner before anything is written down

SEND — access

  • Two rocks, not four — same objective, less to hold at once
  • Table pre-printed; record with ticks and photographs rather than writing
  • Adult at the water station throughout
  • The “because” sentence may be spoken and scribed

Stretch

  • Choose for two different jobs and explain why the answer changes
  • Predict before testing, then account for a prediction that was wrong
  • Use permeable and impermeable in the written reason

Misconceptions to expect

  • “Heavier means harder”
  • “It looks wet, so it soaked it up”
  • “Bigger rocks are stronger”
  • Giving an opinion as a reason — “because it's nice”

Assessment — what to look at

  • The “because” sentence must name a property, not an opinion
  • “Because it's nice” has not met criterion 3, however neat the work is

Why this one is cheap to catch

By the time a worksheet is requested the objective and criteria are already structured data — so it's a plain check, not a judgement call. Which is exactly why it can be trusted, unlike asking a second AI whether a sheet “feels aligned”.

assert worksheet.objective == lesson.objective
for c in lesson.criteria:
    assert any(s.evidences == c for s in sheet)
Worksheets are black and blue in Arial too — the six-colour word-type scheme is gone. The symbols and labels that carried the meaning stay, so it still works photocopied in mono and for a colour-blind child.
